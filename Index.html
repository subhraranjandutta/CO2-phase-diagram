CO₂ phase, density and viscosity

Enter a pressure and a temperature. The page tells you whether pure carbon dioxide is solid, liquid, gas or a supercritical fluid, gives its density and dynamic viscosity, and marks the state on the phase diagram. Methods and references are at the bottom.

Methods and references

Summary. The state of pure CO₂ is classified, and its density and dynamic viscosity are calculated, from two internationally accepted reference formulations: the Span–Wagner equation of state [1] and the Laesecke–Muzny viscosity correlation [2]. All calculations run in your browser and nothing you type is sent anywhere.

1. Phase identification

Phase boundaries are the auxiliary equations of [1], with the following constants and curves.

QuantityValue or equation
Triple pointTt = 216.592 K, pt = 0.51795 MPa
Critical pointTc = 304.1282 K, pc = 7.3773 MPa
Melting curvep/pₜ = 1 + 1955.5390·x + 2055.4593·x², x = T/Tₜ − 1
Sublimation curveln(p/pₜ) = (Tₜ/T)·Σ aᵢ(1 − T/Tₜ)^tᵢ; a = −14.740846, 2.4327015, −5.3061778; t = 1, 1.9, 2.9
Vapour-pressure curveln(p/p_c) = (T_c/T)·Σ aᵢ(1 − T/T_c)^tᵢ; a = −7.0602087, 1.9391218, −1.6463597, −3.2995634; t = 1, 1.5, 2, 4

Below Tt the state is solid above the sublimation curve and gas below it. Above Tt it is solid above the melting curve; otherwise, below Tc, it is liquid above the vapour-pressure curve and gas below it. At or above Tc it is a supercritical fluid when p ≥ pc, and gas below that. A state within 0.1% (in pressure) of a curve is reported as coexistence of the two phases.

2. Density

The residual Helmholtz energy αr of [1] (34 polynomial and exponential terms, 5 Gaussian terms and 3 non-analytic terms) gives the pressure

p = ρ R T [ 1 + δ (∂αr/∂δ)τ ], δ = ρ/ρc, τ = Tc/T (1)

with ρc = 467.6 kg m⁻³, Tc = 304.1282 K, R = 8.31451 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and M = 44.0098 g mol⁻¹. For a given T and p, Eq. (1) is solved for ρ by bracketing and bisection, with the derivative evaluated by forward-mode automatic differentiation. Where the equation has several roots (below Tc) the lowest is used for gas and the highest for liquid and supercritical states.

3. Dynamic viscosity

η(T, ρ) = η0(T) + η1(T) ρ + Δηr(T, ρ) (2)

Here η0 is the dilute-gas viscosity, η1ρ the initial density dependence from Rainwater–Friend theory, and Δηr a residual term in reduced temperature T/Tt and reduced density ρ/(1178.53 kg m⁻³). All coefficients are those published in [2], which covers 100–2000 K for the gas and 220–700 K for compressed and supercritical states up to 8000 MPa along the melting line. The critical-enhancement term of [2] is not included; it matters only very close to the critical point.

4. Verification

The JavaScript implementation was compared with CoolProp 8.0.0 [3], which implements [1] and [2], on 673 fluid states (473 random states at 217–700 K and 0.01–250 MPa, and 200 states at 300–312 K and 6–9 MPa near the critical point). The largest relative deviation was below 10⁻¹¹ for both density and viscosity. This shows that the code reproduces the published formulations; the uncertainty of the underlying correlations is documented in [1] and [2]. For an independent cross-check of any single state, see [4].

5. Limitations

  • The system is treated as pure CO₂. Other components, such as brine, N₂ or impurities, shift the boundaries, the critical point and the transport properties.
  • The melting-curve data cover 217–270 K and pressures up to about 284 MPa, so the curve is extrapolated above that. The equation of state is valid up to 1100 K and 800 MPa; further high-pressure solid forms of CO₂ are not distinguished.
  • Density and viscosity are not computed for the solid. Below Tt the gas density is an extrapolation of [1].
  • The supercritical region is continuous. The lines T = Tc and p = pc that bound it are conventions, not phase transitions.
